San José Tree Removal Permit and Compliance Framework

Current City of San José guidance states that a permit is needed for street trees, heritage trees, ordinance-size trees whether live or dead, and trees of any size on certain multifamily, commercial, industrial, mixed-use properties, or common areas. The exact regulatory pathway can depend on tree classification, property type, condition, location, and current City procedures.

Street Trees

Street trees are associated with the public right-of-way and receive separate City oversight. A tree near a curb or sidewalk should not automatically be classified as either a private tree or street tree without verifying its location and applicable right-of-way information.

Heritage Trees

Heritage-tree status is a special City designation associated with factors such as history, size, species, or unique significance. Heritage status is not established simply because a tree appears large or mature, and heritage trees require special City consideration.

Ordinance-Size Trees

Under current City guidance, an ordinance-size tree on private property includes a single-trunk tree measuring 38 inches or more in circumference at 4 1/2 feet above ground. For a multi-trunk tree, the trunk circumferences measured at that same height are combined, and a total of 38 inches or more meets the ordinance-size definition.

Ordinance-size status is separate from heritage status. On single-family or duplex lots, an ordinance-size tree can require a removal permit even if it is unhealthy or dead.

Property-Type Rules

Single-family, duplex, multifamily, commercial, industrial, mixed-use, and common-area properties should not be treated as interchangeable. Current City guidance requires approval for trees of any size on certain multifamily, commercial, industrial, mixed-use, or common-area properties, with the specific permit mechanism depending on circumstances.

Hazard and Emergency Conditions

A customer reporting a leaning, damaged, or dangerous-looking tree has identified a concern, not necessarily a formally established imminent hazard. Current City procedures may require supporting documentation, including a signed certified-arborist report for certain hazard pathways.

City Authority

The City determines permit requirements, eligibility, conditions, and approval. A contractor, website, estimate, customer statement, or arborist report does not independently guarantee City approval.

Risk Categories Associated With Misuse

Risk 1: Universal Permit Claims

Statements such as “every San José tree needs a permit” or “residential trees never need permits” oversimplify rules that depend on tree classification and property type. Neither statement should be used as a universal marketing claim.

Risk 2: Permit-Exemption Assumptions

Do not assume that a tree is exempt merely because it is small, dead, damaged, unhealthy, or located on private property. A dead ordinance-size tree can still require a permit, and trees of any size can require approval on certain property types.

Risk 3: Street-Tree Misclassification

Treating a possible public right-of-way tree as an ordinary private tree can create regulatory and operational risk. Property ownership and right-of-way boundaries should not be guessed from appearance alone.

Risk 4: Heritage-Tree Misclassification

Large size alone does not prove heritage-tree status. Conversely, heritage designation should not be dismissed because a tree does not appear unusually large. Current City records and appropriate review matter.

Risk 5: Ordinance-Size Measurement Errors

Single-trunk and multi-trunk definitions differ. Measurements should only be represented when legitimately obtained. The City's threshold should never be used to teach people how to alter, prune, or manipulate a tree to avoid regulation.

Risk 6: Hazard and Emergency Overstatement

Statements such as “definitely an emergency,” “no permit needed because it is dangerous,” or “it can be removed immediately” should not be made when the necessary facts and applicable City pathway have not been established.

Risk 7: Property-Type Confusion

Rules for single-family or duplex properties should not automatically be applied to multifamily, commercial, industrial, mixed-use, or common-area properties. Property classification can materially affect the approval process.

Risk 8: Arborist and Contractor Role Confusion

A contractor observation, certified arborist finding, and City determination are different. An arborist report may support an application but does not guarantee approval, and a contractor recommendation is not a substitute for City authority.

Risk 9: Unsafe DIY Content

Tree-removal marketing should not provide detailed chainsaw, felling, climbing, rigging, crane, aerial-lift, electrical-line, or storm-damaged-tree instructions to unqualified property owners. Tree removal can involve substantial fall, impact, equipment, and utility hazards.

Risk 10: AI-Generated Regulatory Errors

AI-generated content can combine another city's ordinance, an outdated San José requirement, an old measurement threshold, incorrect property classifications, or superseded forms and fees. Regulatory statements should be checked against current City information before publication.

What Tree Removal Content Should Not Do

Do not convert a customer description into a regulatory determination. Never infer permit status, heritage status, street-tree status, hazard classification, or removal eligibility without appropriate verification.

  • Do not state that every tree requires a permit or that no residential tree requires one.
  • Do not state that dead, small, damaged, or private-property trees are automatically exempt.
  • Do not call a tree a street tree, heritage tree, ordinance-size tree, or permit-exempt tree without supporting facts.
  • Do not call a reported concern an imminent hazard without appropriate support.
  • Do not promise emergency exemptions, same-day permit issuance, or City approval.
  • Do not imply that an arborist report guarantees approval.
  • Do not treat permit application as permit approval.
  • Do not fabricate tree measurements, property classifications, replacement requirements, or permit status.
  • Do not instruct customers to remove regulated trees before required authorization.
  • Do not provide instructions for avoiding or defeating tree-protection rules.
  • Do not copy another jurisdiction's requirements and label them as San José rules.
  • Do not imply that LJR Tree Services controls City decisions or is endorsed by the City.

Safer Ways to Discuss Tree Removal

Describe the Concern, Not the Conclusion

Use terms such as “reported leaning,” “visible damage,” “dead branches,” “storm-related concern,” or “reported decline” until the condition has been appropriately evaluated.

Identify Permit Screening as a Separate Step

Explain that current City requirements should be reviewed before representing regulated removal as authorized.

Preserve Tree Categories

Street trees, heritage trees, ordinance-size trees, and other private-property trees should remain separate classifications.

Preserve Property Categories

Single-family, duplex, multifamily, commercial, industrial, mixed-use, and common-area properties may involve different requirements.

Use Conditional Language

Appropriate wording includes “may require a permit,” “depending on tree classification,” “depending on property type,” “based on verified measurements,” “where City approval is required,” and “subject to current City requirements.”

Refer Regulatory Questions to Current City Guidance

Marketing content can explain concepts, but official requirements should remain authoritative for permit decisions.

Practitioner Guidance

  1. Confirm the actual property location and jurisdiction. Do not assume San José rules govern a property without verifying jurisdiction.
  2. Determine whether public right-of-way or street-tree status may be involved. Do not infer ownership from appearance.
  3. Screen for possible heritage-tree status. Preserve heritage designation as a separate City classification.
  4. Document tree measurements only when legitimately obtained and relevant. Keep single-trunk and multi-trunk concepts distinct.
  5. Identify the property type before interpreting permit requirements. Residential and nonresidential pathways can differ.
  6. Separate customer-reported symptoms from verified condition or hazard findings. A concern is not automatically a diagnosis.
  7. Determine whether certified-arborist documentation or City review may be required. Do not promise what documentation will establish.
  8. Verify current City requirements before representing removal as authorized. Permit screening should precede definitive approval language.
  9. Keep contractor scope, permit status, replacement obligations, and City decisions separately documented. Each represents a different project issue.
  10. Review regulatory content periodically. Update material changes to City definitions, procedures, forms, and pathways.
